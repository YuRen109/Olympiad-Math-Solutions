# <span id="problem"></span>Problem
Let $a,b,c$ be real numbers satisfying $a<b<c$, $a+b+c=6$ and $ab+bc+ca=9$.  
Prove that  

$$0 < a < 1 < b < 3 < c < 4.$$

# Source

[BMO2 1995 Problems](https://bmos.ukmt.org.uk/home/bmo2-1995.pdf)

# Key Idea
1. Prove that they are all positive.
2. Construct a cubic equation whose roots are $a,b,c$.
3. Apply [the intermediate value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate_value_theorem).

# Solution


### <span id="claim1"></span>Claim 1 ($0<a<b<c$)

$$0 < a < b < c.$$

*Proof.*  

It is apparent that $c>0$, because, otherwise, if $a<b<c \leq 0$, then $a+b+c < 0$.

Now we will prove $b > 0$.  


Suppose that $b \leq 0$. Then $a < b \leq 0$, $c = 6 - a - b = 6+\left|a\right|+\left|b\right|$ and 

$$\left|a\right| \left|b\right| = ab = 9 - c(a+b) = 9 - (6- a - b)(a+b) = 9 - 6 (a+b) + (a+b)^2 = (3-a-b)^2 = \left( \left|a\right|+\left|b\right|+3 \right)^2.$$

By [AM-GM inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM%E2%80%93GM_inequality) we have 

$$ \left( \left|a\right|+\left|b\right|+3 \right)^2 = \left|a\right| \left|b\right| < \left( \frac{\left|a\right|+ \left|b\right|}{2} \right)^2, $$

implying that $\left|a\right|+\left|b\right| < -6$, a contradiction.  
Hence, $b > 0$.  


Next, we will prove $a > 0$.

Since $b+c = 6-a$ and $bc = 9 - (b+c)a = 9 - (6-a)a = a^2 - 6a + 9$, 
$b$ and $c$ are the two roots of the equation $x^2 +(a-6) x + a^2 - 6a + 9 =0$.  
This yields that $(a-6)^2 - 4 (a^2 - 6a + 9) = -3a^2 +12a \geq 0$, or $0 \leq a \leq 4$.  
Suppose that $a=0$. Then $b+c = 6$ and $bc = 9$, which implies that $b = c =3$, a contradiction.  
Therefore, $a > 0$.

$\square$


### <span id="claim2"></span>Claim 2 

$$0< abc \leq 4.$$

*Proof.*  
By [Claim 1](#claim1) we get $abc > 0$.  

Since $0 < a < b < c$ and $a b+ bc + ca =9$, we get $0< 3a^2 < ab + bc + ca =9$, or $0< a < \sqrt{3}$.
Write $abc = a \left[ 9 - (b+c)a \right] = a \left[ 9 - (6 - a)a \right] = a \left( a-3 \right)^2$.  
Define $f: \text{ℝ} \to \text{ℝ}$ by $f(a) = a \left( a-3 \right)^2$. (of cource, $f$ is smooth)   
Then the derivative and the second derivative of $f$ can be calculated as 

$$ f^\prime (a) = 3 a^2 - 12 a + 9 = 3 (a-1)(a-3) \text{  and  } f^{\prime\prime} (a) = 6 a - 12. $$

Since $f^\prime (1)=0$ and $f^{\prime\prime} (1) < 0$ we have that 
$f$ has a [local maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_and_minimum) at $a = 1$, and 
this is the only [critical point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_point_(mathematics)) 
if we further restrict the domain of $f$ to $(0, \sqrt{3})$.  

Therefore, $abc \leq f(1) = 4$.


$\square$

Let $k = abc$.  
Define $g: \text{ℝ} \to \text{ℝ}$ by $g(x) = x^3 -6 x^2 + 9x - k$. (of cource, $g$ is smooth)    
Then $a,b,c$ are the three roots of the equation $g(x) = x^3 -6 x^2 + 9x - k = 0$. 

By [Claim 2](#claim2) we have $0 < k \leq 4$.  

Suppose $k = 4$. Then the function $g(x) =  x^3 -6 x^2 + 9x - 4 = \left( x-1 \right)^2 \left( x-4 \right)$,  
implying that $a = 1 = b= 1 < c=4$, a contradiction.  
Hence, $0 < k < 4$.  

Plugging $x=0$, $x=1$, $x=3$ and $x=4$ into $g(x)$ we get 

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(0) &= -k < 0, \\
g(1) &= 4 - k > 0, \\
g(3) &= -k < 0, \\
g(4) & 4 - k > 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

By [the intermediate value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate_value_theorem) we have 

$$0 < a < 1 < b < 3 < c < 4.$$
